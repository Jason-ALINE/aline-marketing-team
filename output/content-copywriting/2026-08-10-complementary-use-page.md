## H1: You Don't Have to Choose Between Your Laser and ALINE

If your crew already owns a laser alignment system, good — keep using it. This isn't a pitch to replace it.

It's a pitch to add five minutes and one more tool to the job, because there's a gap between "the shaft is aligned" and "the machine is actually going to run right," and a laser only closes half of it.

## H2: What Your Laser Is Already Good At

Laser alignment tools are fast and they're accurate at what they're built to measure: angular and offset misalignment between the coupling halves. If that's the whole job, a laser gets you there.

## H2: What It's Not Checking

A laser reads the coupling. It doesn't tell you:

- **Pipe strain** — whether the piping is pulling or pushing on the pump casing once it's bolted up, which can throw a perfectly aligned coupling out of tolerance the moment the last flange bolt is torqued.
- **Soft foot** — a foot that isn't making full, flat contact with the baseplate, which flexes the frame every time you snug a hold-down bolt and quietly undoes your alignment numbers.
- **Runout** — bent shafts or coupling faces that aren't true, which will read as "misalignment" on a laser even when the shafts themselves are lined up correctly.
- **Bearing clearance issues** — problems inside the machine that no external alignment reading, laser or otherwise, is going to surface.

None of that is a knock on laser tools. They were never built to catch it. It's just outside the job they do.

## H2: How Crews Are Actually Using ALINE

This is the part that's changed. ALINE was built around the reverse-indicator method, and for a long time the pitch was straightforward: reverse-indicator instead of laser.

What's actually happening on real jobs, especially in chemical plants, water treatment facilities, and pipeline work, is different. Millwrights and maintenance crews who already have a laser aren't retiring it. They're running an ALINE tool on the same job, on the same machine, to check what the laser can't — pipe strain in particular, since that's the one most likely to bite a crew *after* the laser already said "good alignment" and everyone's walked away.

Laser handles the alignment. ALINE checks the strain. Same job, same crew, fifteen extra minutes with a tool that doesn't need batteries, calibration, or a line-of-sight setup.

## H2: When to Run the Check

The most common point crews add an ALINE strain check:

1. **Before final bolt-up** — confirm the piping isn't loading the case before you commit to it.
2. **After final bolt-up** — the flange bolts are the moment strain actually gets locked in, so a post-bolt-up check catches what pre-bolt-up can't.
3. **On recurring problem machines** — if a pump or motor keeps drifting out of tolerance for no obvious reason, strain and soft foot are two of the most common culprits a laser alone will never show you.

## H2: Which ALINE Tool for This

You don't need a full reverse-indicator setup to run a strain check alongside your laser work. An **A-1000** covers the bracket and dial-indicator setup most crews use for this — the Starrett 196B1 dial indicator it ships with reads to .001" accuracy. If your team is also doing full reverse-indicator alignment on other jobs, the **A-2000** and **A-3000** combination sets add that capability in the same case. ## H2: Why This Matters for Specs and RFQs

If your work falls under a spec that references **API 686** or **ANSI S2.75** — common on chemical plant, water treatment, and pipeline jobs — a laser-only alignment record doesn't fully document what those standards actually call for. Reverse-indicator checks for strain, soft foot, and runout close that gap. [Link "API 686 / ANSI S2.75 explainer page" → `/api-686-ansi-s2-75-alignment-standards`]

## H2: Bottom Line

Owning a laser doesn't mean you're done. It means you've handled the alignment half of the job. ALINE handles the other half — the part that shows up as a callback three weeks later if nobody checked it.

## SEO finalization (seo-technical-content-specialist, 2026-08-10)

**Slug rationale:** Site convention favors short, descriptive slugs (`/soft-foot-check`, `/alignment-tolerances`), not the draft's placeholder (`/aline-with-laser-alignment`, which reads brand-first rather than topic-first). `/laser-alignment-pipe-strain-check` matches the pattern and front-loads the two real search concepts (laser alignment, pipe strain) over the brand name, which the title tag and meta already cover.

**Title/meta note on keyword strategy:** This page is NOT a target for the two big head terms from the Aug 10 fix list ("shaft alignment tools" / "pump alignment tool" — 3,480 / 3,109 impressions). Those belong on the homepage and `/tool-sets`, where the intent is "buy a tool." This page's intent is narrower and later-funnel — "I already have a laser, what am I missing" — so I deliberately did not force the head terms into the title/meta here. Forcing them in would be padding, not targeting; it wouldn't match what someone searching this page's actual intent typed. This page's SEO value is more topical-authority / internal-linking support than a page-1 bid on its own.

**H1 — no change recommended.** "You Don't Have to Choose Between Your Laser and ALINE" is a strong, on-voice hook and doesn't need a keyword-exact H1 — the H2 structure underneath ("What Your Laser Is Already Good At," "What It's Not Checking") already carries "pipe strain," "soft foot," "runout" for on-page relevance. Rewriting the H1 to cram in a keyword would hurt the page's strongest asset (its voice) for no real ranking gain at this content depth. Flagging this as a judgment call, not asking content-copywriting to revisit.
